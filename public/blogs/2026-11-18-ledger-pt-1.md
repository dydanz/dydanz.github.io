*Part 1 of **Money Doesn't Retry: Building a Ledger That Can't Lie**, a series on the ideas that keep money software honest (idempotency, journaling, event sourcing, async processing, failover), told through a ledger I built and then deliberately broke.*

---

Rina is on a commuter train, topping up her e-wallet with Rp100,000 so she can pay for lunch when she gets off.

She taps **Top up**. The spinner spins. The train goes into a tunnel.

When the signal comes back, the app says: *"Something went wrong. Please try again."*

So she does. She taps **Top up** again. This time it works. Rp100,000 added.

Lunchtime. She checks her bank app. **Two** debits of Rp100,000.

Or one. Or the first top-up actually failed and the bank still took the money. Or both top-ups landed in the wallet and the bank only charged once, which sounds like good news until someone in finance finds the hole at month-end.

From where Rina is sitting, all of those are possible, and she can't tell which one happened. Neither can the support agent she's about to message. Unless the system was built from day one to answer that question, nobody can.

---

## Most software is allowed to retry. Money isn't.

When most software fails, the fix is "try again." If a photo upload fails, you upload it again. If a "like" gets sent twice, you've liked something twice and nobody cares. If a search times out, you refresh. Retrying is cheap, and the worst case is a little mess.

Money doesn't work that way. A retry that runs twice charges someone twice. A retry that never runs means someone got goods without paying, or paid without getting the goods. And "a little mess" in a ledger means a support ticket, then a chargeback, then a reconciliation report that doesn't balance, and eventually a conversation with a regulator.

I spent seven years as a founding engineer at a payments company in Indonesia. The bugs that cost the most were rarely clever. They were almost always some version of Rina's tunnel: a request that half-happened, a retry that the system treated as a brand new payment, two things updating the same balance at the same moment. Fixing them took hours. Fixing the trust they broke took a lot longer.

So this year I did something I'd been meaning to do for a long time. I built a small ledger from scratch, on purpose, to write down the rules I wish I'd had on day one. Then I spent days trying to break it.

## What I built

The project is called [fintech-ledger-playground](https://github.com/dydanz/fintech-ledger-playground). It's a playground in the honest sense: it runs on a laptop with `docker compose`, it isn't anyone's production system, and the point is to see what the ideas actually cost when you build them.

It has four services:

- **auth-api:** register and log in. Not the interesting part, but you need someone to own the money.
- **ledger-api:** the heart of it. Credits, debits, voids and freezes, on an append-only, double-entry ledger.
- **ledger-worker:** the background crew. It ships events to Kafka, finishes transactions that got stuck halfway, checks the books every minute, and archives old records.
- **fraud-engine:** watches the stream of events and freezes an account that tops up too often, too fast.

```
client ──► auth-api ──► Redis (sessions) + Postgres (audit)
service ─► ledger-api ─► Postgres: events + journal + outbox, in one transaction
                          │
           ledger-worker ─┴─► Kafka ledger.events.v1 ──► fraud-engine ──► freeze via ledger-api

browser ─► ledger-console ─► auth-api, ledger-api (reads), Loki (what happened)
```

There's also a web console, the **Ledger Trust Monitor**, and its job is in the name. It doesn't show pretty charts of revenue. It answers one question: *is money being handled correctly right now, and if something went wrong, how did the system recover?*

I wrote it the way I'd want a team to write it: a business requirements doc first, then a technical design, then a development plan, then the code, then load tests and chaos drills that try to break it. About 130 commits over two weeks. Everything I quote in this series (every number, every failure) comes from those documents and those drills.

## The four questions a money system has to answer

Strip away the services and the acronyms, and a ledger has to answer four questions without hesitating:

**1. Did it happen?** When Rina's top-up comes back with an error, there has to be one place that knows for certain whether money moved. Not "probably." Not "the logs suggest." Yes or no.

**2. Can you prove it?** It isn't enough to know the balance is Rp250,000. You need to be able to show every step that got it there, in order, to an auditor who doesn't trust you.

**3. Did it happen exactly once?** The tunnel retry must not become a second top-up. Two purchases landing in the same millisecond must not both spend the same Rp50,000.

**4. What happens when something breaks?** Databases restart. Caches disappear. Message brokers go down for five minutes. Servers freeze mid-thought. The money has to come out the other side correct, and someone has to be able to *check* that it did.

Every principle in this series is an answer to one of those questions:

| Question | Principle | What it means in plain words |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Did it happen? | **Journaling** (an append-only ledger) | Write everything down in ink. Never erase; fix mistakes by adding a correction. |
| Can you prove it? | **Event sourcing** | Your balance isn't a number someone typed in. It's the sum of everything that ever happened, and you can replay it. |
| Exactly once? | **Idempotency** and **concurrency control** | Asking twice gets you the same answer, not a second payment. Two requests for the same money wait their turn. |
| What if it breaks? | **Sync vs. async**, **failover**, **reconciliation** | Do the essential part right now and the rest later, keep working when helpers fail, and check the books constantly. |

If some of those words are new to you, that's fine. Each one gets a full post, starting with the everyday version before any code shows up.

## The one rule everything else follows

If I had to fit the whole design on a sticky note, it would say:

> **It's better to say "try again" than to say "done" and be wrong.**

The requirements doc puts it more formally: the system must *favor strict consistency over availability for write operations*. In practice, that means when the ledger isn't sure it can record a payment correctly, it refuses. It doesn't guess, it doesn't record a "probably," and it doesn't let one part of the system run ahead of another.

Think of a small shop when the till breaks. A good shopkeeper doesn't start writing prices on scraps of paper and hoping it adds up at the end of the day. They put up a sign that says "back in 5 minutes." Customers are mildly annoyed. Nobody gets overcharged, and the books still balance at closing time.

That sounds obvious. It's surprisingly hard to stick to, because almost every shortcut in distributed systems trades a little correctness for a little speed or uptime. A cache that might be stale, a background job that *usually* finishes, a "we'll fix it in reconciliation" note in a design doc. Each one is reasonable on its own. Together, they're how you end up with Rina's two debits.

The rest of the design is about how to keep that rule *and* stay fast and available most of the time. It turns out you can have a lot of both if you're careful about which parts must be strict and which parts are allowed to be relaxed.

## A preview: pulling the plug on the database

Here's the drill that convinced me the design held up, and it's a good preview of the whole series.

I had a test client behave like a real payments caller: 50 credits and debits per second, across 100 accounts. Every request carried a unique key (more on that in Part 4), and whenever the client got an error or no answer at all, it retried with the **same** key every second until it got a final answer. That's Rina in the tunnel, fifty times a second.

Then, in the middle of the run, I killed the database. Not a polite shutdown: `kill -9`, the equivalent of yanking the power cable. It stayed down for 30 seconds.

What happened:

- **866 requests** couldn't be completed while the database was down, for up to 34 seconds each.
- Between them they made **15,303 attempts**, and every one of those attempts got a fast, honest `503 ledger_unavailable` ("I can't do this right now"). The exception was one connection that was cut at the exact moment of the kill. Nothing answered "done" when it wasn't.
- When the database came back, **all 866 succeeded** on retry, with their original keys.
- Afterwards, a checker compared *what the client had been told* with *what the database actually held*. There were no missing transactions, no extra ones and no duplicates. Every balance matched the sum of what the client had been told succeeded.
- One write was caught exactly halfway: the request had been safely saved, but not yet processed, when the plug was pulled. The client got a `202` ("accepted, still working on it"). After recovery, a background job found it and finished it.
- Finally, a full audit of the books (every transaction and every ledger) found **zero mismatches**.

That's the whole series in one paragraph. The database being down didn't cause the problem; failure is normal. The interesting part is that the system **never lied**. It said "not now" when it meant not now, it said "done" only when the money had moved, and it cleaned up the in-between case by itself.

Each piece of that result is a separate design decision, and each one gets its own post.

## It didn't all go this smoothly

I don't want to oversell it. The drills also found real bugs, and those are my favorite parts of the series:

- A Kafka outage made the event shipper hang silently for five minutes, so the alert that was supposed to fire on rising lag only fired because the heartbeat went quiet, and its message blamed the wrong thing.
- A cache outage added about 190 ms to *every single write*, because each one politely waited for a cache that wasn't there.
- A background worker that *froze* (as opposed to crashing) kept its jobs forever, because nothing noticed it had stopped thinking.
- A database feature I used to wake up the event shipper quietly forced every write in the system to commit one at a time.
- And the fraud engine, the thing meant to *protect* accounts, was tripping over the same idempotency rules I'm about to spend a whole post praising.

Every one of those was found by a test that tried to break the system and checked the books afterward. Every one was fixed, and the drills were run again to prove it. I'll show the before and after numbers when we get there.

## How to read this series

I'm writing this for two kinds of readers at once.

If you **don't write code** (you work in product, ops, finance or compliance, or you're simply curious why your bank app behaves the way it does), every post opens with the everyday version of the problem. You can read those sections, skip the code blocks, and still come away knowing *why* these rules exist and what questions to ask your engineers.

If you **do write code**, the second half of each post is the real design: the SQL constraints, the transaction boundaries, the state machine, the failure that proved it mattered, and the numbers. The code is Go and Postgres, but nothing here is specific to either.
