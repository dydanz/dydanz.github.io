*Part 6 of **Money Doesn't Retry: Building a Ledger That Can't Lie**, the last part: failure, recovery, and checking the books.*

---

Back to the tunnel.

Rina taps **Top up**, the train goes underground, and somewhere between her phone and the ledger, something breaks. Maybe it's her signal. Maybe it's the ledger's database, crashing at the exact moment her request arrives.

Five posts have been about making the ledger correct: ink instead of pencil, a history instead of a number, one answer per key, one payment at a time, and letters that always get posted. This post is about what happens when the machinery under all of that fails, and about the harder question that comes after:

**How do you know the money is still right?**

To find out, I ran four drills against the ledger while a test client sent it 50 payments a second, retrying every failure with the same key, the way a careful payments app would:

| Fault | What I did |
| :--- | :--- |
| Postgres | `kill -9` the database, leave it down for 30 seconds |
| Kafka | Stop the message broker for 5 minutes |
| Redis | Stop the cache for 60 seconds |
| Worker | Kill the background worker that holds the jobs |

After every drill, a checker compared what the client had been told with what the database held, and an auditor went through every transaction in the books. Part 5 covered the Kafka drill. This post covers the rest, plus the auditor, plus the load test.

---

## Four honest answers

Start with the smallest unit of honesty: what the ledger says back.

When you ask a shopkeeper whether your order is ready, there are only a few honest answers. "Yes, here it is." "No, we're out of that." "I can't check right now, the computer is down, ask me again in a minute." And "I've written it down, it's being made, it'll be ready shortly."

The ledger has exactly those four:

| Answer | Means | What the client should do |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| `201` | Done. The money moved. | Nothing. |
| `422` | No, and here's why (`insufficient_funds`, `account_frozen`). | Tell the user. Don't retry. |
| `503 ledger_unavailable` | I couldn't do this right now. | Retry with the **same key**. |
| `202` | I've recorded it, and I'll finish it. | Check back, or retry with the same key. |

The rule from Part 1, *better to say "try again" than to say "done" and be wrong*, lives in the gap between the third and fourth rows.

Recall from Part 3 that every write happens in two steps. **Tx1** saves the request as PENDING and claims its idempotency key. **Tx2** does the work. If Tx1 fails, nothing was recorded, and the answer is `503`. If Tx1 commits but Tx2 doesn't finish, the request is safely saved but not done, and the answer is `202`:

```go
// Tx2 on the request path. If it doesn't
// finish, the request stays PENDING (it's
// durable) and the sweeper completes it;
// the client gets 202.
resp, err = s.Process(ctx, ...)
if err != nil {
    return s.pendingResponse(ctx, txID, p)
}
```

### When the ledger truly doesn't know

There's one case where the ledger can't give an honest yes or no: the database connection drops *while Tx1 is committing*. The commit may have landed, or it may not have, and the ledger can't tell which.

This is where Part 4 pays for itself. The answer is still `503`, "ask me again," and the retry carries the same key. If Tx1 never landed, the retry starts fresh. If it did, the retry finds the claimed key: it gets `409 request_in_progress` while the transaction is still pending, and the original answer once it's finished. Either way, there's one transaction, never two.

The ledger doesn't have to know. It only has to make asking again safe.

### The database drill

So here's the drill from Part 1, now that we have every piece to explain it.

| Time (UTC) | Event |
| :--- | :--- |
| 22:24:30 | Postgres killed with `kill -9` |
| 22:25:00 | Postgres started again |
| 22:25:04 | Accepting queries; the last failed attempt |
| 22:25:08 | Nothing left PENDING |

- **866 requests** couldn't complete while the database was down. Between them they made **15,303 attempts**, for up to 34.6 seconds.
- Every attempt got a `503 ledger_unavailable`, except one connection that was cut at the instant of the kill.
- The `503`s came back fast: **95 ms at p99**. The ledger didn't hang, because a missing database fails at once (the hostname stops resolving, or the connection is refused).
- After recovery, all 866 succeeded on retry, with their original keys. No missing transactions, no extra ones, no duplicates. Every balance matched.
- **One write** had committed Tx1 and not Tx2. Its client got a `202`. Which brings us to the sweeper.

## Finishing what was left half-done

Picture a kitchen at closing time. An order ticket is still on the rail, but the cook who took it went home. Someone has to walk the rail, find tickets that have been hanging too long, and either cook them or tell the customer it isn't coming.

That's the **sweeper**. Every 5 seconds it looks for transactions that have been PENDING for more than 5 seconds, and runs Tx2 for each:

```sql
SELECT id FROM ledger.transactions
WHERE status = 'PENDING'
  AND created_at < now() - '5 seconds'
ORDER BY created_at
FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED
LIMIT 100;
```

Part 3 explained why this signal is so clean: Tx2 writes PROCESSING *and* the final outcome in one commit, so a transaction can never be stuck in PROCESSING. A transaction that's been PENDING for a while was interrupted. Nothing else.

Three details keep the sweeper from making things worse:

- **It never races a live request.** The 5-second threshold must be at least as long as Tx2's own time limit, and the config refuses to start if it isn't. So by the time the sweeper picks up a transaction, the original request has definitely given up on it.
- **It re-checks under the lock.** If the request path finished the transaction a moment before the sweeper got to it, the sweeper sees it's no longer PENDING and quietly moves on.
- **It gives up honestly.** A transaction still PENDING after 15 minutes isn't retried forever. It's moved to FAILED with `processing_timeout`, without touching the balance, and its stored answer becomes a `500` that any retry replays. That's an alert, too: it's our fault, not the caller's.

In the database drill, the sweeper found the one half-done write after recovery and settled it. The log line, `ledger.recover stuck_pending`, is the sweeper saying "I found a ticket on the rail and cooked it."

Whether a drill catches a write in that gap is down to timing. An earlier run caught none. So the path also has a test that strands a transaction on purpose and checks the sweeper finishes it, every time.

## Handing over the keys

The sweeper, the outbox relay from Part 5, the reconciler below, and four smaller jobs all run in **ledger-worker**. You want more than one copy of the worker running, so a crash doesn't stop the jobs. But you want each job running in **exactly one** copy, or two sweepers would fight over the same tickets and two relays would post every letter twice.

Think of a small shop with one key, kept on a hook. Whoever takes the key opens the shop. Everyone else checks the hook every few seconds. If the keyholder leaves the building, the key goes back on the hook, and the next person to check takes it.

In the worker, the key is a Postgres **advisory lock**, one per job, held on a database connection:

```go
key := "ledger-worker:" + j.Name
conn.QueryRow(ctx,
    `SELECT pg_try_advisory_lock(
       hashtextextended($1, 0))`, key,
).Scan(&led)
```

A copy that gets the lock runs the job. The others try again every 5 seconds. The lock belongs to the connection, so if the leader's process dies, its connection closes, Postgres releases the lock, and someone else gets it.

There's one more detail, and it matters. The leader pings its own lock connection every second. If that ping fails, it **stops the job at once**, before another copy can take over. Otherwise a leader that had lost its connection, but didn't know it yet, could keep running the relay alongside the new leader.

**The drill:** run two copies of the worker, find the one holding the jobs, and `kill -9` it.

- The other copy took over **all 7 jobs in 2.2 seconds**. The target was 10.
- Payments were unaffected: the worker isn't on the write path.
- Outbox lag peaked at **1.3 seconds**, then went back to normal.

## When it broke: the worker that froze

Killing a process is a clean failure. The process is gone, its connections close, and everyone can tell.

Real systems also fail in a messier way: they **freeze**. A long garbage-collection pause, a deadlock, a virtual machine that the host stops scheduling. The process is still there, its connections are still open, but it's not doing anything.

So I tried that too. I froze the leader with `docker pause` for 60 seconds.

The other copy took over **no** jobs. The frozen worker kept all 7 locks the whole time.

Back to the shop. The keyholder hasn't left the building. They're standing in the doorway with the key in their hand, fast asleep. Everyone checking the hook sees it empty and assumes the shop is open.

From Postgres's point of view, nothing was wrong. The frozen process's network connections stayed open, so there was no reason to end its session. Its once-a-second pings stopped, but the pings were for the *leader's* benefit. Nothing on the database side was listening for them.

For a payments system, this is the bad kind of failure. A frozen leader stops the relay, so the fraud engine stops hearing about payments. It stops the sweeper, so half-done writes stay half-done. It stops the reconciler, so nobody is checking the books. And it does all of that silently, until something else notices.

**This one isn't fixed yet.** In Part 1, I said every problem the drills found was fixed. That was wrong for this one, and for the next. The likely fix is a Postgres setting, `idle_session_timeout`, on the lock connection: a healthy leader pings every second, so its session is never idle, and a frozen one's session would be ended after, say, 10 seconds, freeing its locks. I haven't tested it, and I won't call it fixed until a frozen-leader drill passes.

The lesson: **a crash is the easy failure, because it announces itself.** Every failover design should be tested against a process that stops thinking but doesn't die.

## When it broke: the cache that made everyone wait

The Redis drill passed. Every one of **9,101 writes** during the 60-second outage succeeded on the first try. Idempotency fell back to Postgres, exactly as designed in Part 4.

But look at the latency:

| | p50 | p99 |
| :--- | ---: | ---: |
| Before the outage | 5 ms | 55 ms |
| During | **191 ms** | **235 ms** |

The target for p99 is 150 ms.

Every write talks to Redis twice: once to look for a stored answer before Tx1, once to store the answer after Tx2. Each call has a 100 ms time limit. With Redis gone, every single write waited out both limits, politely, for a cache that wasn't coming back. It also wrote two warning lines each time: **6,148** in a minute at 50 writes a second, which would be about 600 a second at real traffic.

The standard fix is a **circuit breaker**, named after the one in your house. When a circuit fails, the breaker trips, and the house stops sending current down that wire. You don't keep flipping the light switch. Someone checks, and resets it.

In code: after a Redis call fails, skip Redis entirely for, say, 5 seconds, then let one call through to see if it's back. Writes would go straight to Postgres at normal speed, and the logs would say "Redis is down" once instead of twice per payment.

**This isn't built yet either**, and that's partly a decision rather than a delay. A degraded mode that costs 190 ms per write might be acceptable for a 60-second outage. It might not be for a 60-minute one. That's a call for whoever owns the latency target, not something the code should decide quietly.

## The auditor who comes every minute

Everything so far is about the ledger *staying* correct. But every design has bugs nobody has found yet, and every production database eventually gets touched by someone with good intentions and a `psql` prompt.

So the last piece is an auditor that doesn't trust any of the rest.

In a shop, the till says it holds Rp2,450,000 at closing. Nobody takes the till's word for it. Someone counts the cash, adds up the receipts, and checks they agree. Not once a year: every evening.

The **reconciler** does that every minute. It checks four things:

| # | Check | Catches |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | Each balance equals its last checkpoint plus every journal line after it | A balance updated without a journal line |
| 2 | Journal line numbers run with no gaps and no repeats, and every running balance adds up | A missing, duplicated or edited line |
| 3 | Every settled transaction has exactly two lines summing to zero, and every currency's whole journal sums to zero | Money created or destroyed |
| 4 | Every transaction and account matches the replay of its event history | A summary that drifted from its story |

Those four checks are Parts 2 and 3, turned into questions. The journal is the ink, the events are the story, and the reconciler asks, every minute, whether the numbers people actually read still agree with both.

### Reading without stopping the shop

An auditor who locks the doors to count the cash would be no use at a busy shop. The reconciler reads each batch of 500 rows in a single read-only snapshot (`REPEATABLE READ READ ONLY`). That way a balance and the journal lines it's compared with come from the same moment, so a payment that commits halfway through can't make an honest balance look wrong. It takes no locks and doesn't block a single payment.

The every-minute run only checks what changed since the last one, starting from a **watermark**. I first took that watermark from the worker's own clock. Then, during testing, the Docker VM's clock drifted **15 minutes** away from the host's, which would have made the reconciler skip a quarter of an hour of changes without a word. The watermark now comes from the Postgres clock, the same one that stamps every row.

On top of the every-minute runs, there's a **full sweep** of every transaction and every ledger every 24 hours, and also whenever a new worker becomes leader. That second rule is for failover: the every-minute state lives in the leader's memory, so a new leader starts by checking everything.

### It never fixes anything

When the reconciler finds a mismatch, it reports it, pages someone, and **changes nothing**. It checks the same thing again on every run until a human repairs it, so the alert keeps firing until the data is actually right.

That's deliberate. If a balance disagrees with its journal, the cause might be a bug that's still running. An auditor that quietly "corrected" the balance would hide the bug and keep the books wrong in a new way. The repair tools from Part 3, a void or a reviewed `rebuild-projections`, are for people who know *why*.

### Does it catch anything?

An auditor that has never caught anything might just not be looking. So the tests break the books on purpose:

- **A balance nudged by 7** with a direct `UPDATE`: the full sweep reports `balance_mismatch, expected 1001, actual 1008`, keeps reporting it, and goes quiet once it's repaired.
- **A stray journal line** two numbers past the end: `sequence_gap`, plus `unbalanced_transaction` for a line with no transaction behind it.
- **A transaction or account edited** to disagree with its events: `projection_drift`, found by the every-minute run.

And to check the tests themselves, I broke the reconciler on purpose, one way at a time: added the running balance in the wrong order, removed the balance check, dropped the "keep reporting until fixed" rule. A test failed for every one.

One honest gap: a direct `UPDATE` that doesn't change a row's `updated_at` is invisible to the every-minute run. The nightly sweep catches it. That's a day, not a minute, and it's the reason the full sweep exists.

### What it found in the drills

After every drill and every load test, I restarted the worker to force a full sweep. Every one was clean. The largest covered **1,081,550 transactions** and **10,010 ledgers** in 164 seconds, with **0 mismatches**.

## The load test, including the parts that didn't pass

Here's the scorecard from a 30-minute run at 500 writes a second, across 10,000 accounts, with 10 "hot" accounts taking 20 writes a second each:

| Target | Goal | Result | |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Success ratio | ≥ 99.99% | **100.00%** of about 1.07 million writes, nothing left PENDING | ✅ |
| Reconciler afterwards | clean | 0 mismatches over 1.08 M transactions | ✅ |
| Outbox lag | p99 < 1 s | 980 ms (max 2.5 s) | ⚠️ barely |
| Throughput | 300/s sustained, 600/s spike | 514/s offered; 8,462 requests couldn't start on time | ❌ |
| Write latency | p99 ≤ 150 ms | **1.41 s** | ❌ |

Two red rows. Here's what I found when I went looking.

The latency started within target. Then, around 13 minutes in, it climbed to seconds, right when Postgres began writing large amounts of data back to disk. The database's wait statistics showed almost every waiting connection queued behind a single flush of the write-ahead log to disk.

A durable commit has to wait for the disk to confirm the data is safe. On a server SSD, that takes well under a millisecond. On this laptop's Docker virtual disk under sustained load, the disk's own counters showed flushes averaging **60 to 314 ms**.

To test that, I ran the same load with `synchronous_commit=off`, which tells Postgres not to wait for the flush. That's a diagnostic setting only, never for real money, because a crash could lose the last moments of committed payments. With nothing else changed, p99 dropped from **1,419 ms to 50 ms**, and every target passed.

So the ledger code isn't the bottleneck. The disk is. But I can't call NFR-1 and NFR-2 met, because I haven't run the test on a machine that proves it. The verdict in the repo says "not met locally," and it stays that way until someone runs it on a Linux host with a real SSD.

The test did find two real things in the code:

- **The doorbell from Part 5,** a `NOTIFY` that made every commit in the system wait in one line. Fixed.
- **Each write produces about 11 KB of disk log.** The outbox was the biggest table, at **3.7 GB** after one run, because every event is stored twice and marking a letter "sent" rewrites the whole row. That's what the slow disk had to absorb. There are a few ways to slim it down; none has been chosen yet.

## What these drills didn't test

The drills are good evidence, but only for what they covered:

- **Single nodes.** One Postgres with no standby, one Kafka broker. Switching to a standby database or a new broker leader needs a production-like setup, and wasn't tested.
- **Clean faults.** Containers were stopped, killed or frozen. Nobody dropped packets, slowed a disk or skewed a clock on purpose. A network that silently swallows packets, instead of refusing connections, would make requests wait on timeouts instead of failing fast.
- **One laptop.** Every service, the load generator and the monitoring stack shared one machine.
- **And one drill was invalidated** when the laptop went to sleep on battery in the middle of it, leaving a 928-second gap. The drill runner now checks for gaps and refuses to report a result from a run that had one.

## Questions worth asking your engineers

- **"If the database crashes in the middle of a payment, what does the customer's app get told?"** A fast "try again", or "accepted, finishing it", never a "done" that isn't true.
- **"What finishes a payment that was left half-done, and how long can that take?"** There should be a job with a name, a threshold and an alert.
- **"What happens if a background worker freezes instead of crashing? Have you tried it?"**
- **"When a helper like the cache goes down, does every request still wait for it?"**
- **"Who checks that the balances still match the books, how often, and what happens when they don't?"** The right answer involves a page, not an automatic fix.
- **"Which of your performance numbers were measured on hardware like production?"**

## The end of the series

Six posts ago, Rina was in a tunnel, her app said "something went wrong," and nobody could tell her whether the money had moved.

Here's what it took to answer her:

1. **Never erase, only add.** A double-entry journal in ink, where every rupiah has a source and a destination, and mistakes are fixed by adding a correction.
2. **A balance is a story.** Every change is an event, and the present can always be rebuilt from the past.
3. **Asking twice gets the same answer.** Idempotency keys turn the tunnel retry into a replay, and row locks make two payments for the same money wait their turn.
4. **Do what must happen now; post the rest.** One commit for everything "done" depends on, and an outbox for everything else.
5. **Say what you don't know, finish what you started, and check the books.** Honest error codes, a sweeper, one leader per job, and an auditor that never takes the ledger's word for it.

And the drills showed something about the rules themselves. Each part held because it had been tested by someone trying to break it. Each bug in this series, from the `TRUNCATE` that walked past the guard to the worker that froze with the keys in its hand, was found the same way: not by reading the code, but by doing the thing the design said couldn't go wrong, and checking the books afterwards.

That's the real lesson I'd put on the sticky note next to the first one:

> **It's better to say "try again" than to say "done" and be wrong.**
>
> **And a guarantee you haven't tried to break is one you're only hoping holds.**
