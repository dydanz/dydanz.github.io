*Part 2 of **Money Doesn't Retry: Building a Ledger That Can't Lie**: the journal, the oldest idea in this series and still the best defense against a ledger that lies.*

---

Rina, our commuter from Part 1, bought a Rp30,000 coffee that never arrived. She asked for a refund, got it, and opened her e-wallet history to check.

The coffee purchase is still there.

Right below it there's a new line: **Refund +Rp30,000**. The original wasn't deleted, crossed out or edited. Her balance is back where it was, and the history shows exactly how it got there: she paid, and then she was paid back.

Most people never think about this, but it's a deliberate choice, and it's the most important one in the whole ledger. The system could have quietly deleted the purchase. Her balance would be the same. It would even look cleaner.

It would also mean nobody could ever prove what happened.

---

## Ink, not pencil

Picture two ways of keeping a shop's accounts.

**The pencil way:** one number on a whiteboard, "Cash: Rp1,250,000." Every sale, you rub it out and write the new total. It's quick and simple, and the number is always "current."

**The ink way:** a notebook. Every sale gets a new line with the date, what it was and the amount. You never rub anything out. If you make a mistake, you write a *new* line that corrects it.

The whiteboard tells you what you have. The notebook tells you what you have *and how you got there*, and that second part is the whole point. When the whiteboard says Rp1,250,000 and the till holds Rp1,200,000, the whiteboard can't help you. The notebook can: you go back through the lines and find the one that's wrong.

A lot of software keeps money the pencil way. There's a `balance` column, and every transaction runs something like:

```sql
UPDATE accounts SET balance = balance - 30000 WHERE id = 'rina';
```

That's one line of SQL, it's fast, and it looks correct. But the moment it runs, the previous balance is gone. If a bug runs it twice, the database records the result and nothing about the cause. If someone asks "why is Rina's balance Rp70,000?", the honest answer is "because the row says so."

In finance, "because the row says so" isn't an answer an auditor accepts, and it isn't one a regulator, a customer or your own on-call engineer at 2 a.m. can work with either.

## The three rules of the journal

The ledger in [fintech-ledger-playground](https://github.com/dydanz/fintech-ledger-playground) is built on three rules. They're older than computers, and each one is enforced by the database itself, not by good intentions.

**Rule 1: Write in ink.** Journal lines are only ever *added*. Nothing is updated, and nothing is deleted.

**Rule 2: Every rupiah comes from somewhere and goes somewhere.** Each transaction writes exactly two lines, one for each side, and they add up to zero. This is *double-entry bookkeeping*, written down by the Italian mathematician Luca Pacioli in 1494 and used by Italian merchants even before that.

**Rule 3: Number every line.** Each line on a customer's ledger gets the next number in sequence, with no gaps, plus the running balance after it. That makes the history a numbered list you can check line by line, not just a pile of entries.

Let's see what that looks like for Rina.

---

## Rina's journal

Rina tops up Rp100,000, buys the Rp30,000 coffee, and then gets refunded. Here are the journal lines the ledger writes:

| Transaction | Ledger | Amount | Sequence | Balance after |
| :--- | :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Top-up | Rina | +100,000 | 1 | 100,000 |
| Top-up | `topup_clearing` | −100,000 | | |
| Coffee | Rina | −30,000 | 2 | 70,000 |
| Coffee | `settlement` | +30,000 | | |
| Refund (void of Coffee) | Rina | +30,000 | 3 | 100,000 |
| Refund (void of Coffee) | `settlement` | −30,000 | | |

A few things to notice:

- **Every transaction is a pair.** The top-up didn't make Rp100,000 appear out of nowhere: it came *from* `topup_clearing`, the system's record of money arriving from banks and payment partners. The coffee money went *to* `settlement`, the record of money owed to merchants. These are **system ledgers**, the "somewhere" in Rule 2.
- **Each pair sums to zero.** +100,000 and −100,000; −30,000 and +30,000. Add up the entire journal, every line ever written, and you get exactly zero. If you don't, something is wrong, and you know it immediately.
- **The refund is new lines, not an edit.** The coffee lines are still there. The refund reverses them precisely, sign for sign.
- **Rina's lines are numbered 1, 2, 3, each with a running balance.** An auditor can start from zero, apply each line, and check that every "balance after" matches. If line 3 says 100,000 but 70,000 + 30,000 doesn't equal it, the error is right there in line 3.

That last property sounds pedantic until the first time you need it. "Rina says her balance was wrong last Tuesday" stops being an investigation and becomes a lookup.

## Money is an integer

Before any of this, there's a smaller rule that trips up a surprising amount of financial software: **money is never a floating-point number.**

Most programming languages store decimals in binary floating point, which can't represent many everyday values exactly. The classic demonstration:

```javascript
0.1 + 0.2 // 0.30000000000000004
```

That error is invisible in one calculation and very visible across a million of them.

In this ledger, every amount is an `int64` in the currency's **minor unit**. For Rupiah the minor unit is the rupiah itself, so Rp30,000 is stored as `30000`. For US dollars it would be cents, so $12.34 is `1234`. There is no floating point anywhere in the ledger.

Integers have their own failure mode: they overflow. Add two very large numbers and they wrap around to a negative one, which in a ledger means a customer's huge balance suddenly becomes a huge debt. So arithmetic is checked, and it refuses instead of wrapping:

```go
// Money is an amount in the currency's minor unit (IDR has exponent 0).
// There is no floating point anywhere in the ledger.
type Money struct {
	Amount   int64
	Currency string
}

func addInt64(a, b int64) (int64, error) {
	if (b > 0 && a > math.MaxInt64-b) || (b < 0 && a < math.MinInt64-b) {
		return 0, ErrOverflow
	}
	return a + b, nil
}
```

On top of that, a single request is capped at 1,000,000,000,000 minor units, far below half of `int64`'s maximum, so adding any two valid amounts can never overflow. Adding two amounts in *different* currencies is also an error, not a silent mistake.

## Making the rules impossible to break, not just discouraged

Here's where a lot of systems stop: the application code follows the rules, and everyone agrees not to break them. That works until someone runs a "quick fix" script in production, a new service talks to the database directly, or a bug sneaks past code review.

My rule for this project was stricter: **if the ledger depends on a rule, the database enforces it.** The application *should* behave, but the database must make sure.

### Rule 1, enforced: the journal refuses to change

Both source-of-truth tables, the journal and the event log (Part 3), have triggers that reject any attempt to modify them:

```sql
CREATE FUNCTION ledger.append_only_guard() RETURNS trigger
LANGUAGE plpgsql AS $$
BEGIN
    RAISE EXCEPTION '%.% is append-only (% not allowed)', TG_TABLE_SCHEMA, TG_TABLE_NAME, TG_OP;
END;
$$;

CREATE TRIGGER journal_entries_guard_row   BEFORE UPDATE OR DELETE ON ledger.journal_entries
    FOR EACH ROW EXECUTE FUNCTION ledger.append_only_guard();
CREATE TRIGGER journal_entries_guard_trunc BEFORE TRUNCATE ON ledger.journal_entries
    FOR EACH STATEMENT EXECUTE FUNCTION ledger.append_only_guard();
```

An `UPDATE`, a `DELETE` or a `TRUNCATE` on the journal fails with an error. It doesn't matter whether the code was written badly, a script is wrong or a person is in a hurry. In production there's a second lock as well: the application's database role is only *granted* `SELECT` and `INSERT` on these tables, so even disabling the trigger wouldn't give the app the right to change a row.

### Rule 2, enforced: unbalanced transactions can't commit

The "two lines, summing to zero" rule is checked by a **deferred constraint trigger**, which runs at `COMMIT`, after all of a transaction's lines are in:

```sql
SELECT count(*), sum(amount), count(DISTINCT currency)
  INTO n_lines, total, currencies
  FROM ledger.journal_entries
 WHERE transaction_id = NEW.transaction_id
   AND created_at = NEW.created_at;

IF n_lines <> 2 OR total <> 0 OR currencies <> 1 THEN
    RAISE EXCEPTION 'ledger.journal_entries: transaction % is unbalanced (% lines, sum %, % currencies)',
        NEW.transaction_id, n_lines, total, currencies;
END IF;
```

If a bug writes one line instead of two, or two lines that don't cancel out, or two lines in different currencies, the whole database transaction is rolled back. A half-written payment can't exist.

### And the rest

| Guarantee | Enforced by |
| :--- | :--- |
| A user's balance never falls below its floor | A check under a row lock while processing, plus a `CHECK (balance >= -overdraft_limit)` as the last line of defense |
| One user, one account | `UNIQUE (user_id)` |
| A line's currency always matches its ledger's | A composite foreign key on `(ledger_id, currency)` |
| A transaction can only be refunded once | A partial unique index (more on this below) |
| Line numbers have no gaps and no duplicates | Assigned as `last + 1` under a row lock, and checked continuously by the reconciler (Part 6) |

Each guarantee has a primary enforcement and, where it matters, a backstop. The idea is that no single mistake, in the code or in the schema, is enough to corrupt the books.

## When it broke: the TRUNCATE that walked past the guard

I'd love to say all of that worked the first time. It didn't, and the way it failed is the most useful lesson in this post.

The journal is **partitioned by month**: under the hood, `ledger.journal_entries` is really a set of tables, one per month, such as `journal_entries_y2026m11`. That keeps queries fast and, years later, makes archiving clean.

I wrote the guard triggers on the parent table and wrote a test that tried to `UPDATE`, `DELETE` and `TRUNCATE` the journal. All three were refused.

Then, while testing the schema, I tried the same thing against a single *month* directly:

```sql
TRUNCATE ledger.journal_entries_y2026m11;
```

It worked. Every row for the month was deleted, without a single error.

The cause is a Postgres detail that's easy to miss. **Row-level** triggers on a partitioned table are automatically copied to every partition. **Statement-level** triggers, which is what a `TRUNCATE` guard has to be, are not. The parent table was protected; its children weren't.

The fix: the function that creates each new monthly partition now adds a `TRUNCATE` guard to that partition too, and an integration test truncates a partition directly to prove the guard holds. The design doc also writes down what triggers *can't* stop: `DROP TABLE` or `DETACH PARTITION` are schema changes, not data changes, so in production only two dedicated roles (the one that runs migrations and the archiver) are allowed to do them.

The lesson isn't "Postgres is tricky." It's that **a guard you haven't tried to break is a guard you're only hoping works.** The first version of this test checked the front door. The rows were deleted through a side door I hadn't tried.

## Fixing mistakes: the void

If nothing can be edited or deleted, how do you fix a mistake?

The same way an accountant does: you write a correction. In this ledger it's called a **void**. A void is a brand-new transaction that points at the original and writes the exact opposite of its two lines:

| | Rina's line | System line |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Original `DEBIT` (coffee) | −30,000 | +30,000 on `settlement` |
| `VOID` of it (refund) | +30,000 | −30,000 on `settlement` |

The original's status changes to `VOIDED`, the void has its own complete history, and the journal still sums to zero. Anyone reading Rina's history later sees the full story: a purchase, then a reversal, then the reason.

Voids have rules of their own, and each one exists because the alternative is a real-world mess:

**A transaction can only be voided once.** Imagine a support agent clicks "Refund" and their colleague clicks it at the same moment. Two refunds would mean Rina gets her Rp30,000 back twice. The database prevents it with a partial unique index: only one active void may point at any original.

```sql
CREATE UNIQUE INDEX transactions_single_void_key ON ledger.transactions (original_transaction_id)
    WHERE type = 'VOID' AND status IN ('PENDING', 'PROCESSING', 'SETTLED');
```

A second void while the first is in progress or done is refused before it even becomes a transaction. A void that *failed*, though, doesn't block a later attempt, because a failure didn't move any money.

**A void can't break the balance floor.** This is the subtle one. Suppose someone tops up Rp100,000 with a stolen card, spends it immediately, and then the bank charges the top-up back. The natural move is to void the top-up, but the money's gone, and voiding it would push the account to −Rp100,000.

The ledger refuses that void with `insufficient_funds`, just like it would refuse an ordinary purchase. The demo script in the repo plays exactly this scene: the chargeback is refused, the account is **frozen** while it's investigated, and the chargeback settles once enough money arrives to cover it. Voids are still allowed on frozen accounts, because refunding a customer shouldn't depend on whether they're under suspicion.

That rule was an explicit design decision, recorded as an open question in the design doc and answered before any code was written. In finance, "what happens when a reversal doesn't fit?" isn't an edge case. It's a Tuesday.

## Did it hold?

Under the load test, about 5% of all requests voided an earlier transaction: **53,118 voids settled** in a single run. Afterwards, a full audit of the books checked every one of **1.08 million transactions** and **10,010 ledgers**, including the 10 hot accounts with about 36,000 lines each:

- every transaction had exactly two lines summing to zero;
- every currency's journal summed to exactly zero;
- every user ledger's line numbers were gapless, with every running balance correct.

There were zero mismatches. Part 6 goes into how that audit (the *reconciler*) works.

## "Forever" is a long time

One last rule: financial records have to be kept for years. The requirement here is to keep the journal for at least seven years and then move it to cold storage indefinitely.

You can't simply keep growing one database table forever, and you can't `DELETE` old rows either. Rule 1 forbids it. So archiving is its own careful procedure, run month by month:

1. Write a **checkpoint**: each ledger's balance at the last line of that month, so future balances can be computed from the checkpoint instead of from the very first line.
2. **Export** the month to files in object storage that's **write-once**: the bucket's compliance lock means nobody, including the account owner, can delete or change a file before its retention ends.
3. **Read it back** and verify every row count and checksum against a manifest.
4. Only then **detach and drop** that month's table. This is the *only* way ledger rows ever leave the database, and it's a schema operation by a dedicated role, never a `DELETE`.

A month that contains an account under legal hold is skipped entirely. And if the archiver refuses to run because the bucket isn't actually write-once, it says so and archives nothing.

## Questions worth asking your engineers

If you don't write code but you're responsible for a product that moves money, these are fair questions to ask your engineers, with the answers you'd want to hear:

- **"Can anyone edit or delete a transaction record?"** No, and the database itself blocks it, not just the app.
- **"How is a refund recorded?"** As a new, opposite entry that points at the original. The original stays.
- **"If we add up every entry, what do we get?"** Zero, per currency. And something checks that continuously.
- **"Can you show the history behind any balance, line by line?"** Yes, numbered with no gaps, with a running balance on every line.
- **"What happens to the records after seven years?"** They move to write-once storage, verified, and nothing is ever just deleted.

If any of those answers is "well, usually," that's where your next incident is hiding.
